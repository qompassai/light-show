### Exam keyword hook

When the question says **single conductor**, **ferrous metal raceway**, or **inductive heating**, use this path:

`General requirements for wiring methods -> Induced currents in ferrous metal enclosures -> 300.20`

### Mistake trap

- If the question is about **inductive heating in ferrous metal**, think **300.20**.
- If the question is about **surface metal raceway as a wiring method**, think **Article 386**.
- The word **ferrous** can pull you toward the wrong article, but the deciding clue is **induced currents / inductive heating**, which points to **300.20**.

\begin{array}{ll}
\Delta P_{\text{dB}} & \text{Power change / gain (measured in decibels, dB)} \\
P_1 & \text{Initial power level (in Watts, W)} \\
P_2 & \text{Final power level (in Watts, W)} \\
\frac{P_2}{P_1} & \text{The power ratio (equals } 2 \text{ when power is perfectly doubled)} \\
\log_{10} & \text{Base-10 logarithm function}
\end{array}
$$

The 3 dB Rule: Every time you double your power ratio ($\times 2$), you add roughly $3\text{ dB}$. (Conversely, cutting power in half is a loss of $-3\text{ dB}$).

The 10 dB Rule: Every time you increase power by a factor of 10 ($\times 10$), you add exactly $10\text{ dB}$.

$$\alpha \approx k_1 \sqrt{f} + k_2 f$$

$$
\begin{array}{ll}
\alpha & \text{Total Attenuation constant (signal loss per unit length, in dB/m or dB/ft)} \\
f & \text{Frequency of the transmitted signal (in Hertz, Hz)} \\
\sqrt{f} & \text{The component representing Skin Effect losses (dominates at lower high-frequencies)} \\
f & \text{The component representing Dielectric losses (dominates at ultra-high frequencies)} \\
k_1, k_2 & \text{Constant coefficients based on the physical properties of the copper cable and insulation}
\end{array}
$$

For an ideal transformer, current varies inversely with turns:

\[
I_s = I_p \times \frac{N_p}{N_s}
     = 5 \times \frac{600}{150}
     = 20\,\text{A}
\]

\[
\begin{array}{ll}
I_s & \text{Secondary current (amperes, A)} \\
I_p & \text{Primary current (amperes, A)} \\
N_s & \text{Secondary turns} \\
N_p & \text{Primary turns}
\end{array}
\]

## Ampere-Turn Practice Problems

The formula for ampere-turns is:

\[
\text{Ampere-turns} = N \times I
\]

Where:

\[
\begin{array}{ll}
N & \text{Number of turns} \\
I & \text{Current in amperes (A)}
\end{array}
\]

---

### Question 1

A coil has 3 turns and carries 12 A of current.  
What is the magnetomotive force in ampere-turns?

- A) 9 ampere-turns  
- B) 15 ampere-turns  
- C) 36 ampere-turns  
- D) 48 ampere-turns  

**Correct answer:** C) 36 ampere-turns

\[
\text{Ampere-turns} = N \times I
\]

\[
\text{Ampere-turns} = 3 \times 12
\]

\[
\text{Ampere-turns} = 36
\]

### Question 3

A magnetizing coil produces 18 ampere-turns with 6 turns.  
What current is flowing in the coil?

- A) 2 A  
- B) 3 A  
- C) 6 A  
- D) 12 A  

**Correct answer:** B) 3 A

Start with:

\[
\text{Ampere-turns} = N \times I
\]

Substitute the known values:

\[
18 = 6 \times I
\]

Solve for current:

\[
I = \frac{18}{6}
\]

\[
I = 3\ \text{A}
\]
